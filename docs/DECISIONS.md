# Design decisions and evidence

A record of how the current design was chosen (Auburn Senior Design, fall 2026), so a new team
knows what was tested and what was not. The testing code and raw results were removed from the
main branch at handoff; they are preserved in the Git tag `archive/pre-handoff`
(`git checkout archive/pre-handoff` to look at them).

## 1. Embedding model: all-mpnet-base-v2

- Benchmark: 75 research-phrase pairs rated 1 to 5 by three team members (three sets of 25).
  Models were compared by rank agreement with the human ratings (Spearman ρ), never by raw
  cosine values, which are not comparable across models.
- Results: all-mpnet-base-v2 0.829, gte-large-en-v1.5 0.813, e5-large-v2 0.731, bge-m3 0.664.
  The difference between MPNet and GTE is not statistically significant (paired bootstrap
  confidence interval crosses zero).
- MPNet was chosen for the highest observed agreement, 768 dimensions, faster runtime and no
  remote code. The revision is pinned in `matching_pipeline/config/pipeline.json`.
- **Limit:** MPNet was validated only for comparing short scientific phrases (keywords, topics).
  It was not validated for embedding whole documents or retrieving document chunks.
- History: the first prototype used BGE-M3 with one vector per researcher and per opportunity.
  That produced "magnet" opportunities that appeared in many researchers' top matches. In a
  re-creation on the same data, the largest number of researchers sharing one opportunity in
  their top 3 was 12 with BGE-M3 single vectors, 9 with MPNet single vectors and 8 with MPNet
  topic-to-topic matching. Most of the gain came from the model change; topic-to-topic matching
  adds a little more and makes every match explainable.

## 2. Topic generation: Qwen3-4B through Ollama

- Paper keywords and opportunity topics are generated by a small local language model so no
  client text leaves the machine.
- Compared on 25 papers and 24 opportunity excerpts: Phi-4-mini and Qwen3-4B (Phi-tiny-MoE was
  dropped: it needed GPU-oriented remote code and took about 160 s per paper). Both produced
  valid JSON for all 49 inputs. In a blind team review of 10 inputs, 72% of Qwen3-4B's terms were
  rated fully valid versus 60% for Phi-4-mini (overall 4.3 versus 3.9 out of 5); the difference
  came from opportunity text, papers were tied.
- Full runs with Qwen3-4B: 322 papers (about 5.9 keywords each, about 20 s per paper) and 39
  opportunity sections from 22 opportunities (about 5.9 topics each, about 28 s per section).
  "Valid" means the output had the required structure, not that every term was correct.

## 3. Filtering administrative terms: rules, not a model judge

- A second model (Gemma 3 4B) was tried as a judge of generated terms. Against 14 terms the
  team had rejected, it caught 1. A small rule filter caught 11 of 14 and wrongly flagged 1 of
  85 valid terms (optimistic: the rules were written after seeing that review). Gemma 3 12B was
  slower (about 80 s per output) and flagged real chemistry terms.
- Decision: rule filter (`matching_pipeline/src/rules.py`) on the opportunity side only; no model
  judge. On papers the rules mostly flagged real research subjects (e.g. studies of grant
  funding), so paper keywords are not filtered.
- Career-stage topics (e.g. "early-career investigators", K-award recipients) are set aside for a
  future eligibility layer rather than matched as science.

## 4. Opportunity text: section-aware splitting

- Full announcements are mostly administrative. `matching_pipeline/src/sections.py` keeps the
  scientific sections (purpose, background, objectives, scope) and drops award, eligibility,
  submission, review, budget and contact sections. About 17% of announcement words are kept.
- 22 of 25 opportunities have usable text; 3 CDC announcements contain only a title and are shown
  as not matched.

## 5. Matching: topic to topic with evidence (scoring v2)

- Every opportunity topic is compared with every researcher keyword. No single averaged vector
  per researcher or opportunity is built.
- v1 (October 30): pair score = mean of the 3 strongest links; up to 10 topics per opportunity by
  importance.
- v2 (current): topics taken evenly from every section; the 3 evidence links must use different
  topics and keywords; topics that are similar to everything count less in the ranking. The
  displayed similarity is the mean cosine similarity of the 3 evidence links.
- Blind team review of 91 pairs (each version's top 3 per researcher, version hidden from
  raters): v1 and v2 tied (6 of 25 top-1 matches relevant; 10 of 75 top-3 matches relevant).
  v2 was kept because its similarity separates good from poor matches better.
- Cutoff: at 0.50, v2 showed 40 of 75 top-3 matches and kept all 10 relevant ones; none of the 35
  matches below 0.50 was rated relevant. The website shows "No close match" below 0.50 and labels
  matches "Strong match" from 0.55 (`frontend/src/config/matchStrength.js`).
- Caveats: one review pass, small sample, and the cutoff was chosen on the same data it was
  checked on. Several researchers have no relevant opportunity in this mostly biomedical set of
  22 opportunities, which is expected, not a fault of the scoring.

## 6. Rules that still apply

- Cosine similarity is a semantic similarity score, not a probability, a confidence or a
  likelihood of funding. Any percent-style display must be labeled "semantic similarity".
- Eligibility, deadlines, funding amounts and agency belong in a separate rule/metadata layer
  (not built yet).
- Embeddings propose candidates (for example near-duplicate topics); people confirm merges.
- Client data never goes into the repository. Project-created data goes into separate
  `ausme_*` tables; the client's tables are not changed.
